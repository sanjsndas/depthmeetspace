The Poetics of Interior Space: Beyond Decoration

By Elena Marchetti March 14, 2025 15 min read

Interior design is often reduced to a visual exercise — choosing paint colors, arranging furniture, selecting fabrics. But at its most meaningful, interior design is about the experience of space: how a room makes you feel, how it shapes your daily rituals, and how it connects you to the world outside. The best interiors are not decorated; they are composed.

Consider the concept of spatial sequence. In architecture, we talk about the journey through a building — the compression of a hallway before the release of a living room, the framing of a view. Interiors work the same way. A well-designed home guides you gently from one zone to another, using light, proportion, and material to signal transition. This is not about grand gestures; it’s about subtle cues. A change in floor material. A lowered ceiling. A shift in color temperature from warm to cool.

Another overlooked element is acoustic comfort. We design with our eyes, but we live with our ears. Hard surfaces — concrete, glass, stone — reflect sound, creating a sense of coldness and echo. Soft surfaces — textiles, wood, cork — absorb it. The most comfortable interiors balance the two. A room that looks beautiful but sounds harsh will never feel truly restful.

“Good interior design is not about filling space. It is about shaping the silence between objects.”

Finally, consider material honesty. There is a growing movement away from faux finishes and toward materials that age gracefully and express their nature. Solid wood that develops a patina. Brass that tarnishes. Linen that softens with every wash. These materials tell a story over time, connecting us to the passage of years. In a world of disposable design, this is a quiet radical act.

The interior is not a stage set. It is a living environment. When we design with depth — considering sequence, acoustics, and material authenticity — we create spaces that support not just our eyes, but our whole lives.

Reading a Floor Plan: A Practical Guide for Homeowners

By Julian Reyes March 11, 2025 18 min read

Most people look at a floor plan and see lines. Architects see a story: how you will move, where light will fall, what will feel cramped and what will feel generous. Learning to read a floor plan is one of the most useful skills a homeowner can develop — whether you’re buying, renovating, or building.

Start with circulation. Trace a path from the front door to the kitchen, to the living room, to the bedrooms. Are there bottlenecks? Does the flow feel natural? A good plan has clear primary routes and avoids forcing people through one room to reach another. Look for hallways that are at least 36 inches wide; anything narrower feels tight.

Next, examine daylight. Which rooms face south? Where are the windows? In the northern hemisphere, south-facing rooms get the most sun. If a living room has only a north-facing window, it may feel gloomy in winter. Conversely, a west-facing bedroom can overheat in summer. The best plans balance light across the day.

Look at proportion. A room that is 10 by 10 feet feels different from a room that is 8 by 12 feet, even though the area is nearly the same. Square rooms feel more formal; rectangular rooms feel more dynamic. The golden ratio (roughly 1:1.6) often produces the most pleasing proportions.

Finally, consider storage. Where will coats go? Where will the vacuum live? Where will you put the recycling? Good plans integrate storage into the architecture, not as an afterthought. Look for closets, built-ins, and utility spaces that make daily life easier.

Reading a floor plan is not about memorizing symbols. It’s about imagining life within those lines. The more you practice, the more intuitive it becomes.

Choosing Sustainable Materials Without the Greenwashing

By Priya Nair March 6, 2025 14 min read

Sustainability in interior design has become a marketing buzzword. Every product claims to be “eco-friendly,” “natural,” or “green.” But what actually matters? How do you choose materials that are truly better for the planet and for your health?

The first principle is longevity. The most sustainable material is the one you don’t have to replace. A solid wood floor that lasts 100 years has a lower environmental impact than a cheap laminate that fails in ten. When evaluating a material, ask: How long will it last? Can it be repaired? Can it be refinished?

The second principle is embodied carbon. This is the total greenhouse gas emissions associated with extracting, manufacturing, transporting, and installing a material. Materials like reclaimed wood, bamboo, and cork have low embodied carbon. Concrete and aluminum have high embodied carbon. Choosing local materials also reduces transportation emissions.

The third principle is indoor air quality. Many building materials emit volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that can harm human health. Look for low-VOC or zero-VOC paints, adhesives, and sealants. Solid wood and natural stone are generally inert. Engineered wood products may contain formaldehyde binders — check for CARB Phase 2 compliance.

Finally, be skeptical of certifications. Some are meaningful (FSC for wood, Cradle to Cradle for broader materials), but many are self-awarded. Do your own research. Ask manufacturers specific questions. The most sustainable choice is often the simplest: natural, local, durable, and repairable.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between interior design and interior architecture? +
Interior design focuses on finishes, furniture, and aesthetics within existing spaces. Interior architecture involves structural changes — moving walls, altering floor levels, and reconfiguring spatial volumes. Many projects blend both.
How do I choose a color palette for my home? +
Start with what you already own and love. Pull colors from a favorite rug, artwork, or even a view outside. Limit yourself to three main colors plus one accent. Test samples on the wall for at least 48 hours before committing.
Is it worth hiring an architect for a small renovation? +
For a minor cosmetic update, probably not. But if you are moving walls, changing structural elements, or adding square footage, an architect can save you money by avoiding costly mistakes and ensuring the design meets building codes.
What are the most common interior design mistakes? +
1. Pushing furniture against walls. 2. Ignoring lighting layers. 3. Buying matching sets. 4. Rugs that are too small. 5. Forgetting about scale and proportion. 6. Overlooking storage needs.
